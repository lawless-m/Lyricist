# Unadopted — Song Style Spec

A reusable brief for the **Unadopted** style — big beat carrying a British rant: one distorted riff,
a hard breakbeat, and a man reciting perfectly ordinary things at speed until they turn into
something else, which he never notices. Hand this to Claude with the instruction **"song for
Unadopted"** — optionally with a closed set or an arrangement — and it should produce a new,
standalone lyric set sharing the voice, structure and devices below.

**This spec is provisional and deliberately loose, on the Disassembler precedent.** Two songs exist.
The genre, the voice rule, the closed-set method and the fences are settled; the engine below is a
hypothesis. Write songs, then tighten this file to match what the band keeps doing — not the other
way round.

**Where the band came from — it was found, not chosen.** The owner said Laundry was his favourite
musical output and asked what would be adjacent. The diagnosis that followed was that Laundry's
tracks render thin because its spec forbids nearly everything Suno is good at: no guitars, no bass
as backbone, no solos, no key, and a sampler nominally in the lead slot that three separate
instrument probes proved Suno will not put there. The lead part is empty on the record and the
collage has been carrying it. `get-lifted` was written to test one change — keep the collage, give
it a riff — and it worked. `a-six-six-six` followed with two more references supplied by the owner,
Half Man Half Biscuit and The Fall, and the voice came out different enough that it was a different
outfit rather than a Laundry variant.

**Where the name comes from.** An *unadopted* road is a real thing and a piece of ordinary
officialese: a stretch nobody has taken on. It exists, people drive it every day, and no authority
maintains it or is liable for it. Said about a person the same word means nobody claimed them. Both
senses are true of everything this band sings about and **neither is ever stated in a song** — which
is the band's whole method used as its name. The band is unadopted in the literal sense too: it came
out of a Laundry experiment and it is not Laundry.

Eighth spec in the house. Gypsy-emo turns the wound inward and mournful; institutional hardcore
outward and furious; dissociative hardcore sideways and numb; lurker trip-hop inward and silent;
Lucy Might outward and amused; Penny Rich outward and delighted. This one turns it **forward and
oblivious** — the narrator isn't addressing anybody and isn't wounded. He's going too fast to notice
what he's saying.

## The core idea (provisional)

The engine is **the second reading the narrator walks straight past.**

He recites ordinary things at speed. The ordinary things have another sense. The listener gets it
immediately. He never does, and the song never points at it. In `get-lifted` the four words he
chants — charge, draw, lift, bang — are every one of them a police word, and he is using them as a
rave chant. In `a-six-six-six` the A666 sits between the A580 and the A54 and he just reads the
numbers out.

This is the direct inverse of Laundry, and the inversion is the band. **Laundry's narrator sees the
horror and cannot feel it. This one cannot see it and feels great.** Numb-and-aware against
manic-and-oblivious. Same collage mechanics — concrete nouns, missing joins, sound picking the word
— opposite awareness.

Four hard constraints:

- **Never voice the second reading.** Not in an aside, not in the outro, not by a character who
  gets it. The moment anyone in the song notices, two things break at once: the gap collapses into a
  joke, and the shape becomes Disassembler's shouted-anchor drop — a claim and its deflating
  translation — which is spent forty times over.
- **Manic, not numb and not furious.** He is enjoying himself. If he is flat it's Laundry; if he is
  shouting *at* someone it's Purple Dog.
- **It lands.** Hard stop, on the beat or mid-bar. Against Laundry's no-landing rule, and the single
  clearest audible fence between the two bands.
- **Write long.** The established catalogue finding holds here and matters more than anywhere: word
  count is rhythm material, Suno builds the groove out of what it has been given to say, and a thin
  lyric produces a thin track for reasons that have nothing to do with meaning. There are no sparse
  songs in this band.

## The closed set

Every song is built on one **closed set of short words hammered as percussion** — four polysemous
monosyllables in `get-lifted`, three road numbers in `a-six-six-six`. The set is the song. Choosing
it is most of the work, and it must be:

- **Short and hard-consonant.** Sayable at speed by a room. One or two syllables.
- **Real.** A real road, a real word, a real brand, a real bit of officialese. Never invented, never
  approximated. If a song names a thing, find out what that thing actually is.
- **Carrying a second sense nobody voices.** See the constraint above.
- **Fresh every song.** `charge / draw / lift / bang` and `five eight oh / six six six / five four`
  are both spent and logged. So is the polysemy-as-percussion verse method that `get-lifted` runs
  and the dead-retail succession that the six runs — a new song needs a new kind of set, not a
  refill of an old one.

**The palette** is provincial England, concrete and specific: dead retail, road furniture, verges,
vans, lanes, retail parks, garden centres, leisure centres, roundabouts with something on them.
Half Man Half Biscuit's discipline governs it — **the effect is precision, never a punchline.** A
Talbot Horizon on the verge with the bonnet up since March is funny because it is exact. Reaching
for a gag instead of a fact is the failure mode.

## The voice

British, declamatory, ranted, dead on the beat, **no melody anywhere**. First person, present tense,
fast, no gaps. Two rules:

- **States things with total authority and refuses to explain them.** *"Do not ask me about the
  train. I am telling you there is a train."* The Fall's register: the oddly specific asserted as
  evidence, with no case being made.
- **No wink and no irony at his own expense.** He is not in on it. He does not suspect. The comedy
  and the menace are both in the fact that he is completely sincere.

The Mark E. Smith `-ah` line ending is available and is **capped at two per song** — more than that
and it stops being a voice and becomes karaoke, the same trap the Laundry spec has against Lip
Critic's own mantra tic.

**The voice is not fixed to one singer.** `chelsea-girl` is the female-voice test, and the register
is the constant rather than the gender: declamatory, shouted, dead on the beat, no melody, not in on
it. The reference shifts with the singer — The Fall and Half Man Half Biscuit for the male voice,
X-Ray Spex and Poly Styrene for the female one, which is the same delivery aimed at the high street
and was already writing about shopping. **The `-ah` ending is a male-voice device only**; it belongs
to one specific singer and does not transfer.

**Fence against the roster's other women.** Guessed is one thin close-miked voice that withholds;
girlboss is a woman talking fast and entirely in control; hobo is stacked harmony with no lead at
all. This one is loud, oblivious and singing nothing.

## Structure — two arrangements, both in evidence

Pick one per song; they are the band's dial.

**Drop-built** (`get-lifted`). Verse → build that assembles the closed set one word at a time →
drop with the set complete → verse → the set reordered rather than reworded on the second drop →
fastest verse → final drop where the set comes apart → hard stop.

**Unchanging** (`a-six-six-six`). One riff for the entire track, stated in the first bar and never
developed. No drops: the chants are the same riff louder. The vocal runs continuously. This is the
Fall arrangement, and it asks Suno for the one thing it least wants to do.

## The recurring devices (checklist)

- **The closed set as the hook** — bare words, grammar stripped, shouted as percussion.
- **The unvoiced second reading** — the band's engine, above.
- **The build that assembles rather than collapses** (drop-built songs) — the manic inverse of
  Laundry's mechanical-collapse pre-hook lines, where the kits come apart on the way in. Do not
  write a collapse; that slot belongs to Laundry and is full.
- **Reorder, don't reword** — a repeat that returns altered without a word changing.
- **Authority without explanation** — one flat assertion per song that refuses to justify itself.
- **The harmless-sense pass** — a verse that spends each hook word on its most ordinary reading and
  supplies the other by denial. Used once, in `get-lifted`; rotate it rather than repeat it.
- **A riff, and a hard landing.** Both non-negotiable, both fences against Laundry.

---

## Fences

- **Laundry** — the near neighbour and the one that matters. Numb-and-aware vs manic-and-oblivious;
  no lead instrument vs a riff; no landing vs a hard stop; the feed and the warehouse vs the lane
  and the retail park. Do not use Laundry's imperative-list hook opener, its mechanical-collapse
  pre-hook, its flash-of-legibility slot, or `and I'm not even ___`.
- **hobo** — also collage, also lands. But hobo's narrators want everything and are boasting; this
  one is not acquiring anything, he is only reciting.
- **Purple Dog** — furious, addressed to someone, earns a scream. This one is neither angry nor
  addressing anybody.
- **Disassembler** — system text, and a thesis stated outright in the drop. Here the drop asserts
  nothing at all. Also: no X/Y anchor pairs.
- **Ultracoase** — a clean sequenced synth pulse. This is a distorted guitar riff, blown out.

---

## Don't let it calcify

Rotate every song: **the closed set**, **the kind of set** (polysemy, numbers, names, officialese),
**the palette**, and **the arrangement**. Keep: the unvoiced second reading, the density, the
declamatory British voice with no melody, the riff, and the landing. If two consecutive songs pick
the same kind of closed set, the second one is wrong — rewrite it.

## Reference examples

**`get-lifted`** — drop-built, polysemous set. Four words that are all rave words and all police
words, chanted bare, with the second sense never instantiated anywhere. The verses run the same
trick as sound: *"copper in the copper"*, *"boot in the boot, bag in the boot, boot in the bag"*.
Verse 2 spends each hook word on its harmless meaning. **This is the render that established the
band** — the riff arrived and the track was not thin.

**`a-six-six-six`** — unchanging, numeric set. Three real road numbers recited flat, one of which is
the A666, which nobody in the song remarks on. Half Man Half Biscuit supplies the nouns (Bejam,
Rumbelows, Little Chef, the dead retail in the order it actually died); The Fall supplies the
structure and the refusal to explain.
