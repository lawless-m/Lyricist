# Penny Rich — Song Style Spec

A reusable brief for writing songs in the "bargain bluegrass" style — the Dolly Parton lineage by way of the banjo-vaudeville seed: banjo rolls, boom-chuck guitar, mandolin chop, upright bass, a fiddle answering every line, and one bright warm female voice telling you exactly what everything cost. All songs written to this spec are by **Penny Rich**. Hand this to Claude with the instruction **"song for Penny"** — optionally with a win, a dial position or an arrangement — and it should produce a new, standalone lyric set that shares the voice, structure and signature moves below, not a rewrite of the example. With no further steer, pick an unused win, an unused arrangement, and go.

Sixth sibling spec to **gypsy-emo**, **institutional-hardcore**, **dissociative-hardcore**, **lurker-trip-hop** and **lucy-might**. Same house, sixth temperature. Gypsy-emo turns the wound inward and mournful; institutional hardcore turns it outward and furious; dissociative hardcore turns it sideways and numb; lurker trip-hop turns it inward and silent; Lucy Might turns it outward and amused. This one turns it **outward and delighted** — and it is the only spec in the house where the narrator is straightforwardly, publicly *happy*.

Lucy already took winning, so be clear about the difference: **Lucy wins because nothing happens. Penny wins because something tiny happens and it is enough.** Lucy's catalogue contains no events; Penny's is nothing *but* events, all of them small, all of them accounted for to the penny.

**The name is load-bearing and multivalent — keep all its meanings live.** *Penny* is the smallest unit there is: the coin nobody bends down for, penny-wise, in for a penny, a penny saved. It's also the plainest, warmest, most harmless name on the register — nobody is frightened of a Penny, and nobody counts her. *Rich* is an ordinary surname doing an extraordinary amount of work: said together the name is either a boast or a joke, and which one depends entirely on whether you think she's in on it. The real phrase is *penny wise, pound foolish*; hers inverts it. She is rich **in pennies** — rich by counting, by knowing, by having been under the thing on Tuesday with a torch. People hear the name and assume the joke is on her. That assumption is the entire catalogue.

---

## The core idea

The engine of this style is **being underestimated, and letting them.**

Not *and secretly seething* — that's institutional hardcore in a gingham dress. Not *and quietly bleeding* — that's the lurker. She lets them because being underestimated is **useful**, and because correcting people costs more than it's worth, and because — this is the sharp bit — the underestimation is load-bearing for the win itself. If they knew she was counting, they'd count too. The wrong story about her is an asset she's decided not to spend.

The precedent is Dolly's own: she's never been offended by a dumb-blonde joke, because she knows she isn't dumb, and she also knows she isn't blonde. Every part of the presentation is deliberate and every part of it is working.

The defining move — the equivalent of institutional hardcore's denied Pepsi, gypsy-emo's named grave, dissoc's atrocity-beside-an-ad, lurker's disproportionate shrug, Lucy's near-yes — is **the disproportionately large response to the small correct call.** She knew which way the water would go, or which of the two men to marry, or that the ewe would take the second lamb, or which grandchild was lying — and she is throwing a parade about it. It is the exact structural inverse of the lurker's device: that narrator under-responds to something enormous, this one over-responds to something small, and in both cases *the gap is the song.*

**The win is a judgement, not a transaction.** She was right about something, quietly, in advance, and nobody has credited her for it and nobody will. Money is one available surface and it is by some distance the least interesting one — see the territory section below before you reach for a price tag.

Its twin, and the second half of the engine, is **the correction she doesn't make.** Once a song, somebody says the wrong thing about her — kindly, cheerfully, to her face — and she lets it stand. One line, undramatised, no comment underneath it. That silence is where the whole style lives.

The rule that keeps it honest (the equivalent of gypsy-emo's "instrumentation leads the emotion", hardcore's "the trigger must be petty", dissoc's "under-react", lurker's "no self-pity", Lucy's "never cruel, nothing happens"): **the joy is real and the sums are real.** Two hard constraints:

- **No secret bitterness.** She genuinely enjoys this. The win is *actually* a win, the day was *actually* good, and she'd rather have the truck than the credit. If a draft has her smiling through gritted teeth, it's the wrong genre — you've written the furious one and put a banjo on it.
- **She is never the cleverest person in the room out loud.** The intelligence shows only in the *specifics* — the numbers she reels off, the date she remembers, the thing she noticed on the Tuesday. Never in a line where she says so. See the ban below; this is the reflex this spec will develop and it has to be strangled early.

There's a class wound under all of it, worn as cheerfully as a good coat. She knows what things cost because she's had to. Keep it as texture, not a position — it's why she counts, not a point about the economy.

## The crowd is back — and it's wrong

The other five specs each solved the crowd differently. Gypsy-emo is lonely-*together*: a caravan aching in unison. Institutional hardcore is lonely-turned-communal-and-defiant: a room that all got the same verdict. Dissociative hardcore is lonely-together-and-numb: a mantra chanted to noise. Lurker trip-hop has no crowd at all and the absence is the subject. Lucy Might has exactly one other person and you never hear them.

**This style has a full porch, in three-part close harmony, and they have got the story wrong.**

That's the new thing, and it's the best device in the spec. The crowd isn't comforting her (emo), agreeing with her against the world (hardcore), dissolving with her (dissoc) or absent (lurker). They are **on her side, singing along at the top of their lungs, and celebrating the wrong thing.** They think she got lucky. She is letting them. So the shout-back is a happy crowd being sincerely, affectionately incorrect, and the song never once corrects them.

Which means the family's imperative slot becomes **the permission imperative**: two to four words where she hands them the wrong version herself, on purpose, as a chant. *Call it luck. Say I got lucky. Let 'em think it. Keep the story.* Nobody is barking it at her (hardcore), she isn't saying it to herself in the dark (lurker), she isn't offering it across a table (Lucy) — she's putting it in the crowd's mouth and then singing it with them.

Harmony is *wanted* here, which is a first for the house. Stack it. High lonesome close harmony over cheerful words is the whole Dolly tension and it does the work three specs' worth of gang vocals were doing.

## The territory

The seed song was about a discount and that is the single narrowest room in this house. If the catalogue turns into a woman being pleased with herself in shops, the spec has failed. **The engine is a judgement nobody credited, and judgements are everywhere in a life.** Dolly's own catalogue is poverty, jealousy, faith, a dead child, a coat, a man she can't hold onto and a job that's killing her — the joy is in the delivery, not the subject.

Territory this style covers, none of it about a price:

- **Land, weather and animals.** She knew the river was coming up. She knew which field. She's been right about the frost for thirty years and it's still a surprise to everyone.
- **Marriage and who chose whom.** The town thinks she settled. She did the arithmetic at twenty-four and she was correct, and she has never once said so.
- **Work she does that someone else is thanked for.** The books, the rota, the ordering, the thing that would fall over on Tuesday without her.
- **Family.** Who is lying, who is drinking, who needs the money, who will come home. She knew first, she said nothing, she was right. Also: the grandchild she is teaching to notice things.
- **Grief handled competently.** She did all the arrangements. The eulogy thanked her brother. The flowers were hers.
- **Church, committee, village, small institutions.** The woman who does everything and isn't on the list of who does everything.
- **Music itself.** She can play better than the man who's playing. She sings harmony and it's her arrangement.
- **The presentation as an instrument.** The nails, the hair, the giggle, the good blue coat — all deliberate, all working, never once explained.
- **Being fond of the people who are wrong about her.** This is a subject in itself and it's the warmest one available.

**One theme per song.** And across a batch, the transactional corner — bargains, prices, deals — gets **one song at most**, as a light one. It's where the style started, not where it lives.

## Writing the words — show your working

This style's craft section, the way collage-method belongs to dissoc, sparseness belongs to lurker and the deniable line belongs to Lucy.

The discipline is one sentence: **she'll tell you exactly how a thing was done, and never what it took out of her.**

- **Show the working.** Every song shows her method out loud, in detail, cheerfully — which step she skips, which dog she trusts, how she knew, when she knew, what she did on the Tuesday. It is the **only** place her intelligence is allowed to be visible, and it has to be specific enough to be checkable. Numbers are one good tool for this and not the only one: a count of winters, a date, a mileage, but equally a procedure, a sign in the weather, a name she still remembers, the order she does things in. **Don't let every song's working be a sum** — that's how the catalogue collapses back into shopping. This is the direct inverse of the lurker's dated specific: that one uses a fact as a flat bruise, this one uses it as proof she's pleased to hand over.
- **Things, not images.** Bluegrass doesn't do metaphor, it does objects. The coat, the tyres, the glovebox, the church sale, the hammer, the tag, the receipt. Plain nouns, plain verbs, no reaching, no poeticism. If a line could appear in a hymn or an inventory, it's right.
- **Fast, and no wasted syllables.** The words come quick and land on the beat. Short lines. Full sentences. The register is a woman talking at speed to someone she likes.
- **The silent second person, cheerfully.** "You saw me do the math." A *you* who's a neighbour, a husband, the listener, the man at the desk — present, addressed, never resolved. (Rhymes with Lucy's refused pronoun and dissoc's floating one, but warm.)
- **Refuse the grudge.** She names every price and never the cost. No line about what she's owed, what she's put up with, or what they think of her. Withholding *that one thing* is the exact counterweight to how freely she gives everything else.
- **Dry wit, two beats a song.** One joke at her own expense (the tyres cost half what the truck did) and one at the expense of the person telling the wrong story — affectionate, never sharp. Flat delivery. She's not doing a routine.
- **Repetition that changes meaning.** A chorus that means "aren't I lucky" the first time and something else entirely after the break. Shared with lurker and Lucy; used here in the third direction.
- **One line that lands.** As in all five siblings — one line per song that snaps into plain sense. **The construction *"___ where ___ should ___"* is banned across the house** and stays banned. New shape every song: a price stated once, a fact, a thing she noticed, an admission delivered as small talk.

**The dial.** The lineage runs **porch ← → parlour**: fast, funny, banjo-forward and shouted along with at one end; slow, close, mostly voice-and-dobro at the other. Match it to the win. A pure brag goes porch. A win where the wound is nearer the surface goes parlour, and the harmony gets closer and quieter rather than bigger.

## Song structure (the template)

Fast 2/4 bluegrass form, so treat this as a skeleton hung on a band that is having a lovely time. Crucial principle: **the band is faster than she is, and it will not let her dwell.** It doesn't slow for the sad line. It picks the song straight back up. That impatience is a kindness and it's also the character.

1. **Verse 1** — banjo roll, boom-chuck guitar, fiddle answering the line-ends. Voice bright, warm, forward. First person, past tense, told at speed. The small thing and how well it went. Delighted, and already talking to someone.
2. **Pre-chorus** — mandolin chop tightens, bass pushes. Two short lines: the moment of the win itself, with the number in it.
3. **Chorus** — full band, harmony in from the second line. Opens with the **permission imperative** (*"call it luck"*). Carries **the one line that lands**, and ends on the **crowd's wrong version** — the shout-back, sung with total conviction, sincerely incorrect.
4. **Verse 2** — **the working.** The specifics, in detail and at speed: how she knew, what she did, in what order, and the small checkable facts that prove it. Then **the correction she doesn't make** — somebody says the wrong thing about her, in their words, and she lets it go by in one line with no comment attached.
5. **The plain line and the break** — the signature move. **The band stops dead behind her. She says one line, flat, no vibrato, no repeat** — the true thing under all the sunshine, offered like small talk. Nobody in the song reacts. Then **the banjo starts and takes it away**: banjo, fiddle, mandolin trading eight bars each, fast and happy, and by the time it's over she's back on the chorus and she never comes back to that line. The refusal to dwell *is* the song.
6. **Final chorus** — biggest, full three-part harmony, the crowd singing the wrong version with her. **One word changes** and it's the ending. Then the **hard bluegrass tag**: everybody hits the last note together and stops.

The **plain line and the break is the signature move** — the direct equivalent of gypsy-emo's half-time-to-double-time bridge, institutional hardcore's mock-calm-monologue-that-detonates, dissoc's voice-eaten-by-the-machine dissolve, lurker's drop-out and Lucy's near-yes. It's the moment the arrangement and the subject become the same thing: she tells the truth once, to a room that is already clapping, and the band carries her past it before anyone can feel sorry for her. **Don't skip it, don't let more than one line into it, and don't let her explain it afterwards.**

**The ending is the other inversion, and it matters.** Gypsy-emo ends on a crowd shout, and the other four all refuse to land — dissoc cuts or loops, lurker lets the crackle run out, Lucy has the trumpet not finish its phrase. This one **finishes**. Clean tag, everybody together, dead stop, no fade. The only tidy ending in the house belongs to the person nobody's counting.

## The recurring devices (checklist)

- **The permission imperative as chorus opener** — two to four words handing the crowd the wrong story (*"call it luck"*, *"say I got lucky"*, *"let 'em think it"*). The family's imperative slot at its most generous. (Rhymes with gypsy-emo's *play it quick*, hardcore's *sit down, shut up*, dissoc's *part it out*, lurker's *be nice about it*, Lucy's *sit closer*.)
- **The disproportionate parade** — a full-size celebration of one small correct judgement. Never apologise for the scale of it.
- **Show the working** — her method, out loud and checkable, at least twice a song. Sums are one way; don't let them be the only way.
- **The correction she doesn't make** — someone's wrong version of her, quoted in their words, let past in one line, no comment.
- **The crowd that's wrong and welcome** — close harmony, total conviction, celebrating the luck. Never corrected.
- **The one line that lands** — one per song, plain sense. Never the banned *where/should*.
- **The plain line and the break** — band stops, one flat line, banjo takes it away, never returned to.
- **The silent second person** — a warm unresolved *you*, addressed throughout.
- **Dry wit, two beats** — one at her expense, one at the wrong-story-teller's, both affectionate.
- **Repetition that changes meaning** — the chorus meaning something else after the break.
- **The hard tag ending** — everybody together, stops dead, no fade.
- **Major, and it means it.** Where gypsy-emo says harmonic minor, dissoc says blown-out-and-clipping, lurker says dusty-and-unresolved and Lucy says major-with-a-flat-somewhere, the note here is **open G, honest major, no irony in the chords.** The tension is between the happy key and what's underneath it, and the key must not give it away.

Save the size for the final chorus and the break. **The size here is the joke** — it's a parade for a bargain, and the band means every note of it.

## Handling the subject matter

- **No villains.** The people who underestimate her are neighbours, family, the man at the auction. They like her. They're just wrong about her, cheerfully, for their whole lives. Making them contemptible turns the song into a revenge fantasy, which is a different and worse genre.
- **No reveal, ever.** She never pulls the mask off, never gets her moment, and nobody is ever sorry. **Ban the last-verse turn** where they all find out. If a draft ends with her being finally recognised, delete the ending — the point is that recognition never arrives and she has priced that in.
- **No leaflet.** One woman, one win, one wrong story. Not "women get overlooked" — *Cyril thinks I caught somebody's eye, and I said I must have, and I said it kind.* The particular is the only honest unit here as it is in the lurker spec.
- **She's a little smug and that's allowed.** She's not a saint and not a martyr. She's enjoying herself. Let her.
- **She'd do it again, and she will, next Saturday.** No arc, no lesson, no growth. The catalogue is a run of good days.

**Banned constructions** (this spec's version of institutional hardcore's *"I'm not paranoid — I'm just the only one still ___"*): any line where she names her own position. **No *"they think I'm just a ___"*, no *"I let them think it"*, no *"never underestimate a ___"*, no *"they don't know what I know"*.** All of it is the narrator reading you her own thesis off a card, and this spec will reach for it every single time if you let it. If a draft names the label, **cut the line and put a sum in its place.** The permission imperative is the only place the arrangement is ever acknowledged, and it's acknowledged as a chant, not a confession.

## Reference example (a finished song in this style)

**"Say I Settled"** — Penny Rich. Twenty-two years of a marriage the village has always been slightly sorry about. Sits mid-dial, tipped porch. The win is a judgement she made at twenty-four; the wrong story is that she took what she could get.

```
[Verse 1 — fast, banjo roll, boom-chuck guitar, fiddle answering the line ends. Bright warm female lead vocal, forward and on the beat.]
They gave us a toaster and a look
Ada Pye said "well, she's happy" like a question
I was twenty-four and I had all my sums done
and I married the quiet one

He is not much to look at from the road
and that's the kindest way the village puts it
He can put a roof on. He can put a lamb right.
He has never once been late.

[Pre-chorus — mandolin chop tightens, bass pushes, harmony steps up behind]
Twenty-two winters and the door still shuts
and I have never had to ask him twice

[Chorus — full band, close three-part harmony in from the second line]
say I settled
go on and say I settled
he learned my mother's bread the year we buried her
and never told a soul but me
say I settled, girls
say I settled

[Verse 2 — same tempo, dobro added, fiddle sits out]
He does the gate in March and the gutters in the fall
he knows which step to skip and which dog not to trust
Three grandchildren know his whistle before mine
and I put that down as a win

Marjorie says I'd have had my pick
Marjorie says I could have gone to Leeds
I said I daresay. And I said it kind.
She'll be telling it at my funeral.

[Chorus — identical words, harmony wider]
say I settled
go on and say I settled
he learned my mother's bread the year we buried her
and never told a soul but me
say I settled, girls
say I settled

[The plain line — the whole band stops dead. She says one line, flat, no vibrato, and does not repeat it.]
He'd have been all right without me. I'm the one who wouldn't.

[The break — banjo comes straight back in at full tempo, no sentiment, nothing slows down. Banjo, then fiddle, then mandolin, eight bars each, fast and happy. She does not return to that line.]

[Final chorus — biggest, full three-part close harmony all the way through, everybody singing the wrong version with her]
say I settled
go on and say I settled
he learned my mother's bread the year we buried her
and never told a soul but me
say I settled, girls
say I chose

[Tag — hard bluegrass ending. Everybody hits the last note together and stops. No fade, no ring-out.]
```

**The ur-text:** *"40% Off"* — written by Suno to a bare "banjo vaudeville" prompt, before any of this existed. It's the origin of the whole spec and it already contains the parade, the silent second person (*"you saw me do the math"*, *"you called it lucky luck"*), the receipt kept like a trophy, and the crowd-ready chant. What it doesn't have is the plain line and the break, or the correction she doesn't make. It stays in the catalogue as the earliest and simplest thing she ever recorded.

Companion pieces written to the same spec:

- **"High Water"** — she told them in February and they moved the machinery in May, two days after she'd stopped mentioning it. Pulled to the parlour variant, slow and close, dobro and one distant fiddle. Permission imperative: *"nobody asked me"*. The working is a sign in the hedges her father taught her. The plain line is about the year the water took something of hers, said once, and the band doesn't slow down for it either.
- **"Ada Pye's Flowers"** — her mother's funeral, all of it arranged by her, and the eulogy thanking her brother for stepping up. Countrypolitan, big and bright and absolutely not sad. Permission imperative: *"let him have it"*. The correction she doesn't make is delivered in the receiving line with a plate of sandwiches in her hand. The wit beat is what her brother wore.
- **"Two Sugars"** — eleven years on the tea trolley at the depot, where nobody stops talking when she comes in. Fast porch. Permission imperative: *"nobody minds me"*. The working is a list of what she knows about six men who have never learned her surname. The plain line is what she could do to that place in an afternoon.
- **"Call It Luck"** — the transactional one, and the catalogue only gets the one. A farm auction, a truck for four hundred, and a Tuesday under the bonnet with a torch that nobody knows about. Full banjo-vaudeville variant, fast and rowdy. Permission imperative: *"call it luck"*. The correction she doesn't make is Cyril deciding she caught somebody's eye. The plain line is why they let her stand at the front.

## Don't let it calcify (rotate the surfaces, keep the bones)

Institutional hardcore had to ban its own reflex, dissoc had to ban three, lurker banned the family's shared nod outright, and Lucy's trap was that an undefeated narrator has no arc. This style's trap is the same as Lucy's with an extra hazard: **a narrator who always wins and is always cheerful about it will write the same song forever, and the win will keep shrinking into a gimmick.** The fix is to rotate the day and never the woman. Across a batch, these **must** change song to song:

- **The theme.** One per song, from a different room of the territory each time. **If two songs in a batch are about money, the second one's wrong** — that corner gets one song and no more.
- **The win.** One small correct judgement per song, never the same shape twice — a reading of weather, a reading of a person, a choice made years ago, a thing nobody else spotted, a job done properly while somebody else was thanked.
- **The permission imperative.** Fresh every time. *"Call it luck"* belongs to "Call It Luck" and stays there.
- **The wrong story, and who tells it.** New person, new misunderstanding, always affectionate.
- **The working.** New method, new proof, new kind of knowing. Don't let every song's working be a number, and never let it be a price twice.
- **The plain line.** New shape each time — sometimes an admission, sometimes a fact, sometimes something she'd have to be a different woman to say twice. **Never a conclusion**, and never one she comments on.
- **The one line that lands.** New shape every song. Never the banned *where/should*.
- **The dial and the arrangement.** Porch, vaudeville, parlour or countrypolitan, matched to how near the surface the wound sits.

What *should* recur — these are the style, not tics, and cutting them breaks it: the real delight, the working shown out loud, the correction she doesn't make, the crowd that's wrong and welcome, the band that won't let her dwell, and the clean hard ending. **Rotate the surfaces; keep the bones.** If two consecutive songs share a shape, the second one's wrong — rewrite it.

---

## When writing a new one

Pick **one room of the territory**, then **one small correct judgement** inside it and **one wrong story** about how she came by it. Pick your point on the **dial** (porch ↔ parlour) and your **arrangement** to match how close the wound is running that day. Find the **permission imperative**, the **working** and the **plain line for the break**, and build outward from those three; they're the load-bearing ones. Then take out every line where she tells you what she is, and put a piece of her method in its place.

Keep the whole arc: fast and delighted up front, the chorus that hands the crowd the wrong version, the receipt and the uncorrected correction in verse two, the one flat true line the band refuses to dwell on, and the hard tag ending with everybody hitting it together. Above all keep it **happy, specific and uncorrected** — the joy is real, the sums are real, nobody ever finds out, she'd do it again on Saturday, and the song ends by finishing properly rather than by fading away. If a draft gives you a grudge, a reveal, a victim or a lesson, you've written one of the other five — and if it gives you a shop, you've written the seed song again.
