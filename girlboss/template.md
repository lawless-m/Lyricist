# references-available

**Alias:** `references`, `refs`, `available`, `girlboss`

---

## The persona

A woman, first person, present tense, talking fast. The setting is interchangeable — she
might be fronting a band, running a social media company, selling a house, minding someone's
dog, giving the toast at a wedding. The setting supplies the stakes and the vocabulary. Her
nature supplies the song, and her nature is this: **she wields her sex as a plaything.** Not
a wound, not a currency she's been reduced to, not a strategy she'd admit to having — a toy
she owns outright and takes out whenever a room gets interesting, mostly because she can.

Whoever is in the sightline — a groom, an investor, a sound engineer, a rival's husband —
becomes the instrument. They get no real dialogue and no qualities beyond their
deterioration in her presence: the dropped mug, the lost thread mid-pitch, the meeting that
can't end. Their reactions are the scoreboard. What she wants is never them; it's the
current that runs through a room when she proves the power is hers.

She is not pathetic and she is not punished. This is the load-bearing distinction. She gets
away with it — every time, in every world. The listener is her accomplice, addressed
directly, and is expected to find her delightful — because she is.

She has no interiority. She does not reflect, does not learn, and never catches herself. Any
line where she glimpses her own behaviour from outside belongs to a different band.

## The emotional core

There isn't one, and that's the spec. No longing, no loneliness under the swagger, nothing
damp. What replaces feeling is **nerve**. The pleasure on the page is her own audacity: she
enjoys her nerve more than she enjoys anyone she aims it at, and she reports both at speed.

One situation the nerve can run through — not the definition of the band, just its richest
early seam — is another woman's trust with a man inside it (the babysitter, the bridesmaid).
When a song uses that triangle, the trusting woman stays specific and human and off-stage,
and at the top of the dial the power gets proven on both parties separately, neither ever
learning about the other. But the triangle is a premise, not the engine. The engine travels:
a label boss, a boardroom, a live broadcast, a regulatory hearing will each do fine.

---

## The implied result

The signature move, and the discipline that makes the whole band work: **the song suggests
it worked — and "worked" means what you think it means — without ever saying it.**

- No line, in any phrasing, ever reports the act. "So we had sex" is the one sentence this
  band cannot say, however it's dressed. Not coyly, not crudely, not in past tense at the
  reception.
- Instead, one flat detail placed after the cutaway lets the listener do the arithmetic:
  the meeting that ran forty minutes over with nothing in the minutes, his tie different at
  the reception, the soundcheck nobody asks about, the spare key that came back in the
  morning. The listener's own assembly of it is the punchline, and it only detonates if she
  never touches it.
- The detail must be deniable on its own — innocent out of context, damning in it — and she
  never glances at it twice. She reports it the way she reports the weather, then moves on
  to being thanked.

## The raunch dial

This band is raunchy and should read raunchy — brazen, crude, unmistakable, **not radio
friendly**. The calibration is: name anything, stage nothing.

- **Acts can be named.** An offer, a proposition, a memory, a plain crude noun — one line,
  said with total confidence, then move on. The nerve is in how casually it's said, not in
  what follows. Profanity is fine and should sound like hers.
- **Acts are never depicted.** No anatomy in action, no blow-by-blow, no scene. The cutaway
  comes the beat after the offer. Naming is a line; describing is a scene; the scene is
  what breaks a platform's ToS, and it's also where the joke dies — a rendered act is just
  porn admin, but an offer hangs in the air forever.
- The double-entendre engine runs underneath: a professional task delivered slowly is filth,
  and a song can live entirely there ("Happy to Help" does). The named-act songs sit further
  up the same dial, not on a different instrument.
- The dial runs from **deniable** (nothing quotable, everything understood) to **brazen**
  (the offer made out loud, in words, once). Pick a position per song. At the brazen end the
  secrecy moves: it's no longer deniability with the participants, it's that *nobody else
  will ever know* — the room next door, the shareholders, the diary all stay innocent.

## Structure

- **Verse 1** — establish the world and her standing in it: the job, the room, the rules,
  itemised with total professional competence (she is, infuriatingly, genuinely good at the
  job). First deployment lands here, small.
- **Hook** — chanted, two lines maximum, a phrase from the setting's own vocabulary (a
  service line, a slogan, a KPI, a stage call) turned filthy by everything around it.
- **Verse 2** — escalation. The deployment proper: the inventory, the sightline, the
  instrument deteriorating on the scoreboard. At the brazen end, the named offer and the
  cutaway live here.
- **Hook**
- **Bridge — the interruption.** The straight world arrives mid-transgression — whoever owns
  the stakes rings: the bride, the board, the label, the licensing officer. She answers
  without missing a beat and is fluent, elaborate, and longer than the lie needs to be. Two
  registers running simultaneously (what she's saying / what she's doing). Centrepiece;
  best-written section.
- **Verse 3 or hook out** — the implied-result detail, dropped flat and unexamined, then
  the clean exit: the world thanks her and asks her back, none the wiser.

Sixteen-bar verses. Long lines, conversational, heavy internal rhyme, end-rhyme optional and
often deliberately dropped for a punchline.

## The recurring devices (checklist)

- **The itemised world** — whatever the setting trusts her with, rendered in objects: the
  laminated list, the term sheet, the set list, the keys. Competence established through
  inventory before anything is subverted.
- **The instrument** — whoever's in the sightline, reduced to scoreboard reactions. No
  dialogue worth quoting, no qualities beyond uselessness in her presence. When the premise
  is a triangle, the trusting woman is never mocked and never described unkindly — the
  cruelty is structural and stays implicit.
- **The cutaway** — offers can be made in full, kisses can land, but the song cuts on the
  beat after; nothing is staged and nothing is reported. Whether it happened is never said
  on the page — it's implied afterwards, by one flat detail (see the implied result), and
  the day proceeds beautifully either way.
- **The implied result** — see its own section. One deniable detail after the cutaway;
  the listener does the arithmetic; she never does.
- **The task double-entendre** — one professional verb owns each song and gets slower and
  worse as it repeats. Watering, soundchecking, onboarding, staging the house.
- **The inventory** — a rapid flat list: what she wore, where she stood, what he ruined
  trying not to look. Delivered as accounting.
- **Specificity as the joke** — the exact fabric, the exact time the meeting was meant to
  end, the exact lipstick shade. Never "underwear"; always the French ones, the ones you
  don't buy for yourself.
- **The interruption, handled** — the stakes-owner's call, answered mid-crime, the lie more
  detailed than it needs to be.
- **The unearned moral** — she draws a lesson from events that indicts her further and which
  she clearly considers flattering.
- **The flat deadpan** — one line per verse that lands by being said completely straight.
- **The clean exit** — the last line is the world asking her back.

**Never:** remorse, consequence, jealousy, a sympathetic beat, a line that acknowledges the
gap, a staged act, an aftermath, or a stated cause.

## Premise bank

Rotate the world every song; never the same setting twice running, and never two triangle
premises in a row — the triangle is a flavour, not the format.

Singer working a label boss for the single's release slot · founder pitching a funding
round · estate agent at the open house · TV weather presenter and the outside-broadcast
crew · sommelier at the trade tasting · driving instructor's star pupil · hotel duty
manager on the night shift · MP's aide during the count · yoga teacher with a waiting list ·
babysitter (the dad, the lift home) · house-sitter (the husband works from home) ·
dog-walker with a key · wedding planner · cleaner with her own key · dress fitter (the
husband holds the pins) · new lodger

Spent: **the bridesmaid double** (girlboss/maid-of-honour) — corridor offer, make-up room
kiss, flawless toast. Don't rewrite it in a new coat.

## Reference example — *Happy to Help*

> **[Verse 1]**
> She left on the Friday with the list laminated:
> feed the fish, do the post, here's a number if the boiler goes.
> Item nine, underlined: *my husband works from home — just work around him.*
> Oh, I'll work around him. All the way around him. Nice and slow.
>
> **[Hook]**
> Happy to help.
> Whatever needs doing — happy to help.
>
> **[Verse 2]**
> I've been watering her garden in the little white sundress,
> and it's thirsty work whenever his meeting's running long.
> His camera's off, his mic is off, his blinds are doing something funny,
> and the hose takes two hands, honestly, you'd be amazed how much can go wrong.
> I hang my washing on her line — the good stuff, the French stuff,
> the kind you don't buy for yourself, if you're following me at all.
> He came out to ask if I needed anything. Twice. In new shirts.
> I said that's so kind, and let the question sort of... hang there. Like the washing. Till the fall.
>
> **[Bridge]**
> She rang at six — *how's the house, how's the fish, how's everything* —
> and I gave her the fish in some detail. The fish are thriving. Genuinely.
> The garden's never been so watered, the beds immaculate, I have been
> *so* on top of absolutely everything — and your Michael? Barely seen him. Keeps to his room.
>
> **[Out]**
> She's booked me for August.
> Says the place always feels so loved.

Note the out: two facts, no arrow — the implied result doing its work. This example is one
premise (a domestic triangle) at the deniable end of the dial; "Maid of Honour" is the same
band at the brazen end. Neither setting defines the band — she does, and the worlds rotate.
Keep new examples clean of the shared trope library's anchors — run the standard checks
before a draft leaves the folder. (This example predates the "twice" retirement note in the
library — don't imitate that line's count word.)

## What this band is not

**Not `guessed`.** Its exact inverse; read them against each other. The `guessed` narrator's
defining move is catching herself mid-act. This narrator has no capacity for that and the
band dies the moment she acquires one. If a draft produces a self-observation line, it is a
`guessed` line in the wrong folder; move it or cut it.

**Not `the-bell-knows-my-name`.** No confession, no real loss, no thesis. Bell earns its
emotion; this band refuses to have any.

**Not `coase-guard`.** There is an implicit politics here — power, desire, who actually runs
any given room — and it must stay implicit. The band never states it. If a line articulates
the idea, it belongs upstairs.

**Not a seduction-with-feelings song.** No pining, no falling, no discovery, no man who
matters. The moment she wants a *person* rather than the current in the room, it's a
different band's song.

## Don't let it calcify (rotate the surfaces, keep the bones)

Across a batch, these **must** change song to song:

- **The world** — a new setting every time; never two triangles running.
- **The task double-entendre** — a new professional verb each song; the watering is spent.
- **The hook phrase** — a fresh line from each new world's own vocabulary.
- **The implied-result detail** — new evidence each time: a tie, a timesheet, a returned
  key, a meeting with nothing in the minutes. Never the same detail-category twice.
- **The garment/inventory item** — never the same fabric or list twice.
- **The lie in the bridge** — new shape each time: over-detailed, over-helpful, or one
  perfect omission.
- **The scoreboard beat** — a new thing the instrument ruins trying not to look.

What should recur — these are the style, not tics: the speed, the chanted hook, the itemised
competence, the instrument on the scoreboard, the cutaway, the interruption handled
fluently, and the result that arrives as evidence with no arrow drawn. **Rotate the
surfaces; keep the bones.**
