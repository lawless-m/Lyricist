# hobo — Song Style Spec

**Alias:** `hobo`, `junk swagger`, `the scavengers`, `90s hip-hop guitar crossover`

The 90s hip-hop / guitar crossover lane: a fuzz riff over a live breakbeat, cheap sampled junk on
top, and — the part that actually defines the band — **no lead vocal anywhere**. Stacked female
voices sing the same lyric in close harmony from the first line to the last.

Hand this to Claude with *"another song in the style of this document"* and it should produce a
new, standalone lyric set sharing the voice, structure and moves below — not a rewrite of the
example.

---

## The core idea

The engine is **junk swagger**: collage as *appetite*. Every image is something they found, and
they want it.

This is Laundry's method run at the opposite temperature, and the inversion is the whole band.
Laundry's images drift past a narrator who has stopped being able to feel them; hobo's pile up in
front of narrators who want all of them. Same mechanic — concrete nouns, missing joins, sound
picking the word — opposite charge. **Laundry's collage is a symptom. hobo's is a boast.**

Three consequences that must hold:

- **No comedown.** No rue, no irony at their own expense, no dawning awareness that the pile is
  worthless. The moment they suspect it, you've written a different band.
- **The joins are missing because they're moving too fast**, not because meaning dissolved. The
  drift has a cause and the cause is greed.
- **The song lands.** Against Laundry's no-landing rule: hobo ends hard, on the riff. They win.
  No fade, no loop left running.

---

## The unhinged rule

Sweet delivery, deranged content, and **the gap between them never closes.** The harmony stays
beautiful, in tune and completely untroubled while what it's singing goes past ordinary bragging
into something nobody sane would commit to at that volume: a birthday cake in an unplugged
freezer, a curtain rail bought for a wall that doesn't exist, the haul addressed as though it can
hear them.

Two constraints stop it becoming whimsy:

- **The pitch never wavers.** No screaming, no strain, no wink to the listener. If the voices
  sound unstable the joke is gone — the comedy is that they are *fine*, in harmony, and wrong.
- **Escalation past sense, once per song, somewhere fresh.** Not a fixed slot and not a punchline:
  somewhere in every song the claim gets bigger than the object can hold, and the harmony agrees
  with it anyway. It's never challenged, corrected or resolved — there is no second voice
  available to challenge it, which is the point of having no lead.

One woman insisting a broken lamp is priceless is a character. Five women agreeing about it in
three-part harmony is a **cult**, and the unanimity is what makes it funny and slightly
frightening at once.

---

## The voice

**First person plural, always. Never "I".** A gang of women who have collectively decided the
junk is treasure, singing as one, nobody dissenting. Name individuals inside the gang freely
(Trish has the trolley, Sandra found the curtain rail) — but they are named from inside the
"we", never given their own line to sing.

**No lead vocal at any point.** Close harmony, same words at the same time, sweet and tight and
in tune. Not a frontwoman with backing. Not call-and-response. Not a shouted terrace chant.
Verses are sung in harmony exactly like the hooks — the only thing that changes across sections
is the arrangement underneath.

**What that costs you, and it's the main craft constraint: harmony forces melody, and melody
caps syllable density.** There is no motormouth verse in this band and no rapping. The collage
runs *slow and repeated* — images held, sung, doubled back on — rather than Laundry's fast
clipped image-stream. Every line has to be singable in unison by five people: fewer syllables,
open vowels, and room for a phrase to come round again because it sounds good, not because it
means more the second time.

---

## Writing the words

The lyric doesn't have to add up, and shouldn't. Two rules do most of the work:

- **Alliteration picks the word.** Choose for consonant runs and open vowels before choosing for
  meaning — five voices in unison make a hard consonant land like a drum hit, so the words are
  partly percussion. (Laundry has a phonetics-lead rule too, pointing the other way: there it
  produces near-malapropisms that erode sense. Here it produces something chantable. Same rule,
  opposite outcome.)
- **The nonsense refrain.** One invented phrase per song — alliterative, meaningless, sung in
  full harmony as though it were the title hook of an enormous single. Never explained, never
  glossed, **never reused**: a fresh one every song, logged in the trope library like any other
  device.
  - **Build it from real words in a daft order.** A coined word is exactly the shape of a
    producer tag, and tags get takes silently removed. The genre's founding example is all real
    words; keep it that way however odd the combination.
  - **It grows, it never erodes.** Laundry's mantra degrades into fragments and noise. hobo's
    refrain arrives intact and gets *bigger*. Chopping it would be borrowing the wrong band's
    signature.

Beyond that, plain non-sequitur is welcome as texture — a line needn't follow from the one before
it, provided every image is still a physical object they want.

**The palette fence.** hobo takes roadside, verge, skip, car-boot and gutter junk. Laundry keeps
retail, shipping, conveyor belts and body parts. Object for object, this is the main thing
keeping the two collages apart — if a draft reaches for a returns desk or a good kidney, it's
drifting into Laundry. (Washing machines are Laundry's by name as well as by palette. Leave them
alone.)

## The device pool

Four moves, **rotated, not mandatory.** Each song hits **at least two**, and **never the same two
as the previous song** — a fixed per-song device is exactly what this pool exists to prevent.

- **The haul** — an inventory of found junk sung as a **round** rather than rattled off:
  harmonised, repeated, stacking the way the junk does.
- **The drop-out** — guitar cuts dead mid-hook, breakbeat left bare or gone entirely, and what's
  in the hole is **unaccompanied harmony**. Riff slams back in. With no lead voice able to step
  forward, this is the band's loudest available gesture.
- **The appraisal** — a whole verse spent pricing one broken object, wrongly, at length, sung
  beautifully and with total confidence.
- **The trade** — a line where something genuinely valuable goes out for something worthless and
  they reckon they've done well out of it. **Careful here:** The Bell Knows My Name owns
  *"I still think I came out ahead of that bargain"* (see trope library), so the trade can never
  be scored in that phrasing. Say it some other way, every time.

---

## The bones (never rotate)

The swagger register with no comedown. The unhinged rule. First person plural. All-harmony, no
lead vocal, ever. Collage assembled by appetite rather than drift. The fuzz riff as backbone.
The hard landing.

---

## Song structure

1. **Intro** — fuzz riff, breakbeat drops in, no vocal.
2. **Verse 1** — full harmony, sweet and tight. Establishes the find.
3. **Hook** — carries the nonsense refrain. The riff locks to it.
4. **Verse 2** — same harmony, thicker. The images stack and the unhinged rule bites.
5. **Rotating device slot** — whichever pool move this song is using.
6. **Final hook** — riff doubled, harmony widened, refrain at full size.
7. **Hard stop on the riff.**

Sections are told apart by **arrangement, not by who's singing.** The vocal is the same stacked
harmony throughout, so the riff, the breakbeat and the drop-outs do all the structural work a
lead/backing contrast would normally do. Paste the bracketed tags into Suno — they matter more
here than in the other bands for exactly that reason.

## Reference example

**"Free Means Ours"** — a freezer taken off a verge. Uses **the haul** and **the appraisal**;
deliberately leaves the drop-out and the trade unused, which is the rotation rule working.

```
[Intro — fuzz guitar riff, breakbeat drops in, no vocal]

[Verse 1 — full female harmony, no lead voice, sweet and tight]
There's a freezer on the verge and the verge belongs to nobody,
felt-tip on a bit of card and the bit of card says FREE.
Trish has got the trolley. Trish has always got the trolley.
We walked it up the hill and we have never walked so slowly.

[Hook — the haul, sung as a round, riff locks to the list]
A freezer and a fan and a folding fishing chair,
a lampshade with a life in it, a bootful of brass,
(second voices enter a bar behind, same words)
a freezer and a fan and a folding fishing chair —
FREE MEANS OURS!
wally wally wonder bin,
wally wally wonder bin!

[Verse 2 — same harmony, thicker, the images stack up]
Sandra found a curtain rail. We haven't got a curtain.
Sandra found another one. We haven't got a wall.
We have not plugged it in. It's cold already.
We put a birthday cake in it. There isn't a birthday.

[The appraisal — harmony holds, riff drops to one chord underneath]
Now this. This is a carriage clock.
This is brass. You can tell by the weight of it.
It doesn't go. It doesn't have to go.
Sixty quid. Eighty. A hundred with the hands on —
and the hands are in the freezer,
so a hundred.

[Final hook — biggest, riff doubled, harmony widened, all voices]
A freezer and a fan and a folding fishing chair,
a curtain rail, a curtain rail, a cake and a clock,
FREE MEANS OURS!
wally wally wonder bin,
wally wally wonder bin,
(wide, everybody, full throat)
WALLY WALLY WONDER BIN —

[Hard stop on the riff. No fade.]
```

**On the example:** every device in it is now logged and spent. "Free means ours", "wally wally
wonder bin", the freezer, the carriage-clock appraisal and the birthday-cake escalation are all
retired — the *slots* stay, the fillings don't.

## What this band is not

**Not `laundry`.** Its exact inverse; read them against each other. Laundry's narrator can't feel
the objects, hobo's want them. Laundry withholds catharsis and refuses to land; hobo lands hard.
Laundry decoheres on purpose; hobo is nonsensical out of enthusiasm. One preacher against five
women in harmony.

**Not `girlboss`.** girlboss's narrator is knowing, covert, competent and singular, and her engine
is trust-transgression. hobo is plural, loud, transparent and working nobody — nothing is being
got over on anyone, they're simply delighted with a broken lamp. The vocal architecture is the
hard fence: girlboss is one dry voice in front of the beat, hobo has no lead at all.

**Not `coase-guard`.** No politics, no friend/enemy line, no crew discipline. Both bands sing
"we", but Coase Guard's is an outfit with an enemy and hobo's is a gang with no opponent
whatsoever. They're outside the thing, not against it.

**Not a song about poverty.** The engine is an appetite, not an argument. Nothing here is *about*
being poor or homeless, and no song should read as commentary on either. If a draft starts
pitying them, it's dead — they're having the best day of their lives.

## Don't let it calcify (rotate the surfaces, keep the bones)

Across a batch, these must change song to song — none is a template to refill:

- **The nonsense refrain.** Fresh every time, no exceptions. This is the band's most calcifiable
  surface because it's the most fun to write.
- **The device pair.** Never the same two moves as the previous song.
- **The junk itself.** A new haul every song — the verge-freezer, curtain rails and carriage
  clock are spent.
- **The named gang members.** Trish and Sandra are used.
- **The escalation.** A fresh way for the claim to outrun the object; not always an appliance,
  not always a container.
- **The rotating style texture.** Per `styles.md` — don't let one become part of the locked core
  by accident.

What *should* recur, because it's the style rather than a tic: the all-harmony plural voice, the
appetite-collage, the unhinged register, the fuzz riff, and the hard landing. **Rotate the
surfaces; keep the bones.** If two consecutive songs share a phrase-shape, the second one's
wrong — rewrite it.

---

## When writing a new one

Start from a fresh **haul** (what did they find, and where) and a fresh **nonsense refrain**.
Pick your **two devices** from the pool, checking what the last song used. Then write it slow and
singable: short lines, open vowels, alliteration ahead of sense, the images stacking rather than
connecting.

Keep the whole arc: sweet harmony throughout, the refrain arriving intact and leaving bigger, one
escalation past sense that nobody corrects, and a hard stop on the riff. Above all keep it
**delighted** — not numb, not knowing, not sad. If a draft gives you a comedown, a moral, or a
lead vocal, you've written the wrong band.
